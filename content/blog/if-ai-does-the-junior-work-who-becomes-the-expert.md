# If AI Does the Junior Work, Who Becomes the Expert?

A few weeks ago, an AI agent made the same careful change across thirty of our repositories in about fourteen minutes.

It didn't get tired. It didn't need a break. It didn't need anyone to explain it twice.

It also didn't learn a single thing.

That last part stayed with me.

Because this was exactly the kind of work we used to give someone in their first job. And exactly the kind of work where people quietly learned how everything fits together.

**The agent doesn't need to learn. But someone on our team, five years from now, will.**

## The first step is getting harder to reach

This isn't just our story.

In the jobs most exposed to AI, Stanford found **19% fewer** young people working than expected. Mostly not because they were let go. Because they were never hired.

This month, a survey of more than 8,000 hiring managers found that **26%** are hiring fewer juniors because of AI. Only **7%** are hiring more.

For a lot of young people trying to start a career right now, this is not an abstract trend. It is the job they applied for and never heard back from.

## Nobody becomes an expert by watching

Ask an experienced engineer how they learned, and the story often begins with: *"When I was junior, I got something badly wrong…"*

That's how it usually works. You do the small task. You make a mistake. Someone more experienced sits next to you and explains why. After enough of those moments, one day you're the one doing the explaining.

Junior work was never only about getting tasks done. **It was the training program. We just never called it that.**

Take it away, and you don't only remove a job. You remove a path.

An Anthropic study this year shows the cost. Juniors who learned with AI scored **50%** on a later test. Those who learned without it scored **67%**. They were weakest at finding mistakes, the very skill you need to check an AI's work.

So we have created a strange situation:

**We are using AI to skip the work that turns beginners into experts, right when we need experts to keep an eye on the AI.**

In our own projects, every mistake an agent made was caught by someone with years of experience. They didn't download that experience. They built it, mostly on junior work.

## Some companies are betting the other way

Not everyone is cutting.

IBM said this year it would **triple** its entry-level hiring in the US. Its reasoning: companies that stop hiring juniors save money today, but will struggle to find their future leaders tomorrow.

IBM didn't simply bring back the old junior job, though. It redesigned it around what AI still can't do well: working with real clients, handling the cases the chatbot gets wrong, making judgment calls.

That is the real choice. As one expert put it in the Northeastern report, AI can either hollow out the bottom of the career ladder or turn it into an escalator.

The escalator could look like this: juniors review the agent's work instead of typing it. They explain *why* something is right, not just ship it. They sit next to seniors when decisions get hard.

![A junior reviews the agent's work while a senior colleague explains.](/images/journal/junior-learning-with-ai.png)

It takes a little more time. But it costs far less than a future with no experts.

AI can do the junior work.

It cannot become the senior.

**Someone still has to.**
